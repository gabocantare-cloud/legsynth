# Gait metric definitions — and where we disagree with the paper

Wang (2026), arXiv:2606.22129, reports five gait numbers for the Jansen baseline in Table 4
but never publishes the formulas behind them. That makes his row unreproducible by anyone
else, including us. This page fixes that from our side: it writes our formulas down, shows
how much each number moves when the one judgement call is changed, and states plainly which
of his numbers we can reproduce and which we cannot.

Everything here comes from `legsynth/metrics.py` and is regenerated by
`python scripts/gait_report.py`, which also writes `results/gait_metrics.json`.

---

## 1. The one judgement call: where does the ground begin?

A Jansen foot never sits *exactly* flat. Along the bottom of its path it sags and rises again
by a couple of hundredths of a millimetre. So "the foot is on the ground" is not something you
read off the geometry — you have to draw a line and say *below this counts*. Every number
downstream of that line moves when you move it.

**Our definition.** Let the foot path have total height `H = max(y) - min(y)`. The **ground
line** sits a fixed fraction of that height above the lowest point:

```
y_ground = min(y) + band * H          band = 0.01  (our default)
```

**Stance** is the longest unbroken arc of the crank revolution spent at or below `y_ground`.
"Unbroken" matters: the arc is allowed to wrap past crank angle zero, and a stray dip
elsewhere in the cycle does not get counted as a second foot-fall.

**Why a fraction of the path height and not a fixed 0.5 mm.** Because a fraction is
scale-invariant. Build the same linkage at twice the size and the duty factor, the flatness
and the ripple come out identical while the step length and the clearance simply double —
which is what "the same leg, bigger" ought to mean. A fixed millimetre tolerance has no such
property: it would call a desk-sized model twitchy and a full-sized Strandbeest flat, for the
exact same geometry. This is enforced by `test_metrics_are_scale_invariant_where_they_should_be`.

**Why 1% and not something else.** 1% of path height is 0.225 mm on the Jansen baseline. Below
about 0.4% the band stops measuring the stance at all: the bottom of the Jansen path has two
shallow low points, so an over-tight band splits into two separate contact arcs and the
contiguity rule then reports one dimple instead of the stroke. Above a few percent the band
starts eating into the lift-off and touch-down curves, and "stance" begins to include parts of
the swing. 1% sits in the flat middle of that range. It is still a choice, which is why
Section 3 reports how the numbers move rather than hiding it.

`ground_line(path, tol=...)` takes an absolute millimetre band instead, for anyone who wants
to compare against a fixed-tolerance convention.

## 2. The five formulas

Let `x, y` be the foot path sampled at `n` crank angles spaced uniformly over one revolution,
`S` the stance mask defined above, and `|v|` the foot speed **per radian of crank angle**
(central difference on the periodic sample). Using speed per radian rather than per second
means the crank rate cancels out of every ratio, so no motor speed has to be assumed.

| Metric | Formula | What it means physically |
|---|---|---|
| **Step length** | `max(x_S) - min(x_S)` | How far the body is pushed forward in one crank turn, assuming no slip |
| **Ground clearance** | `max(y) - y_ground`, which is exactly `(1 - band) x H` — see below | The tallest obstacle the leg can swing over without stubbing |
| **Duty factor** | `(crank angle in stance) / 2π`, i.e. `count(S) / n` | Fraction of the turn with the foot planted. At duty `d` a machine needs at least `1/d` phase-shifted legs to always have a foot down |
| **Stance flatness** | `std(y_S) / step_length` | RMS wobble of the foot while planted, made dimensionless. Multiply by step length and you get, in mm, how much the body bobs per step. Lower is better |
| **Velocity ripple** | `std(|v|_S) / mean(|v|_S)` | Coefficient of variation of foot speed over the ground stroke. The planted foot is the body's only link to the ground, so an uneven one makes the machine surge and drag at constant crank speed. Lower is better |

Duty factor being a plain sample count is only valid because the path is sampled uniformly in
crank angle — which is what `JansenLeg.foot_path` returns, and what `metrics.py` documents as a
precondition.

**Ground clearance is an identity, not a fifth independent observation.** With the ground line
at `min(y) + band × H`, where `H = max(y) − min(y)` is the path height:

    clearance = max(y) − y_ground = H − band × H = (1 − band) × H

So at our 1% band the clearance is exactly 99% of the path height — 22.232376 against
22.456946, a ratio of 0.9900000000 — and it scales linearly and exactly with the band rather
than being merely "bounded above" by the path height. Three consequences worth carrying:
a statement about clearance is a statement about path height; the 0.85× clearance constraint
in the optimization is a 0.85× path-height constraint; and the argument below, that 25.7 mm
of clearance cannot come from a 22.46 mm path, is exact rather than a bound. The identity is
pinned to 1e-9 by claim `A1` in `scripts/verify_docs.py`. Passing `tol` (a fixed tolerance in
millimetres) instead of `band` breaks it, because a fixed tolerance does not scale with the
path.

## 3. Our numbers vs the paper

Jansen holy numbers, branch `(-1,-1,1,-1,1)`, **1440 crank samples**, band 1%
(= 0.2246 mm). Foot path: 67.91 mm wide × 22.46 mm tall.

1440 is `metrics.N_PUBLISHED`, the one sample count behind every table in this repo.
It matters here more than it looks: stance is the longest unbroken *arc* inside the
band, so its measured extent is quantised by the sample spacing, and step length
reads 43.41 mm at 1440 against 43.49 mm at 3600. Neither is wrong. Quoting one here
and the other in the README, which is what this document used to do, is.

| Metric | Ours | Paper (Table 4) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Step length | **43.41 mm** | 43.3 mm | +0.3% |
| Ground clearance | **22.23 mm** | 25.7 mm | −13.5% |
| Stance flatness | **0.0011** | 0.0281 | −96% |
| Velocity ripple | **0.0920** | 0.0956 | −3.8% |
| Duty factor | **31.2%** | ≈20% | +56% |

Two of the five reproduce well. Three do not, and they do not fail in the same way.

## 4. The finding: no single stance definition reproduces Table 4

The sensitivity table above says the band matters. The useful question is the inverse one:
**take each of the paper's numbers in turn and ask what band our code would need in order to
produce it.** If his row came from one consistent definition, the four bands would land on
roughly one value, and that value would be his tolerance recovered.

They do not.

| Paper's number | Band our code needs to reproduce it |
|---|---|
| Duty factor ≈20% | **0.35%** (0.078 mm) |
| Step length 43.3 mm | **0.98%** (0.220 mm) |
| Velocity ripple 0.0956 | **1.27%** (0.286 mm) |
| Stance flatness 0.0281 | **31.2%** (7.00 mm) |
| Ground clearance 25.7 mm | **unreachable at any band** |

The required bands span a factor of **90**. Step length and velocity ripple agree tightly at
about 1% of path height — that is a real reproduction, and it is why we adopted 1% as the
default rather than the 0.5 mm this repo used before. But no single line drawn anywhere on
this foot path yields the paper's duty factor *and* its step length: 20% duty needs a band
about three times tighter, and at that band the step length falls to about 36 mm. His stance
flatness needs a band 30 times looser, at which point "stance" covers three quarters of the
revolution and is no longer a ground stroke in any physical sense.

Two further observations, offered as evidence rather than accusation:

- **Ground clearance cannot be a definition problem at all.** Clearance is `(1 − band) × H`
  exactly, and this path is `H = 22.46 mm` tall, so clearance is *at most* 22.46 mm and is
  22.23 mm at our band. No stance rule and no choice of band can extract 25.7 mm from it;
  only a taller path can. The obvious remaining
  explanation — that the paper measured a linkage with slightly different link lengths — was
  chased separately and **refuted**: see §8 of [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md). Briefly, a
  perturbation below the holy numbers' own 0.1 mm precision does land the clearance at
  25.71 mm, but it costs 5% of the step length; and the linkage that hits both geometric
  numbers exactly sits 23.8% from Jansen with a velocity ripple 58% from the paper's. The
  geometry side reaches the same verdict as this page does from the stance side.
- **The duty factor is the only entry in Table 4 written as "approximately".** Every other
  value carries three significant figures. A plausible reading is that ≈20% is a quoted
  Strandbeest design convention carried into the table rather than a number measured by the
  same pipeline as the rest of the row. We cannot verify that, so we state it as a hypothesis
  and leave the disagreement standing.

**We are not tuning our band to close these gaps.** The 1% band was chosen on the
scale-invariance and fragmentation arguments in Section 1, before the comparison, and it is
the band the whole repo uses. Where the paper's numbers follow from it, we report agreement;
where they do not, we report the disagreement. `test_no_single_band_reproduces_the_papers_table`
pins this claim in the test suite, so if it ever stops being true the tests fail and this page
gets rewritten rather than quietly drifting.

## 5. What this means for the rest of the project

The optimization in Step 7 constrains step length, clearance and duty factor to stay at or
above 0.85× the Jansen baseline. Those constraints are now referenced to **our** measured
baseline, computed with the definitions on this page, not to the paper's Table 4. Mixing the
two would compare designs measured with one ruler against a threshold set with another.

Concretely, the baseline the optimizer defends is: step length 43.41 mm, ground clearance
22.23 mm, duty factor 31.2%, at band = 1% and 1440 crank samples.

The one thing that carries over unchanged is the paper's *relative* claim — that Jansen is
Pareto-dominated on gait quality versus wear. Ratios between designs measured with a single
consistent definition survive a change of definition far better than absolute values do, which
is the honest reason his headline result can still be checked even though his baseline row
cannot be reproduced.
